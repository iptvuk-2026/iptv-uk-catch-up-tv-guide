What catch-up TV is, and what it is not

Catch-up TV is simple in principle: after a programme has been broadcast live, it remains available to watch again for a set period. You open the programme guide, find the show you missed, and press play. There is no need to have recorded it in advance, no need to set a timer, and no need to be sitting in front of the television when it first airs. If you were out, at work, or simply watching something else at the time, the programme waits for you.

It is worth being clear about what catch-up is not. It is not the same as live television, because you are watching a programme that has already finished. It is not the same as a library of films and box sets, which are available for months or years rather than days. And it is not the same as recording, which depends on your own device having storage space and having been told in advance what to save. Catch-up sits between live TV and on-demand libraries: a short window after broadcast in which a programme can be watched again, before it moves on. How catch-up works on IPTV

On a traditional recorder, catch-up depends on your own box: it saves the programme to a hard drive while it airs, and you play it back later. On IPTV, the work usually happens on the provider's side instead. When a channel broadcasts a programme, the provider keeps a copy on its own servers for the catch-up window. When you choose that programme from the guide, the server sends it to you as a stream, just as it would a live channel. Your device does not need a hard drive, a recording schedule, or any spare storage at all.

That server-side approach is what makes catch-up so convenient, but it is also why quality varies so much between providers. Keeping copies of every programme on every channel, for days at a time, takes serious storage and organisation. A provider that takes catch-up seriously will have the systems to do it properly: programmes appear promptly after they finish and stay available for the full advertised window.

The guide itself is the key to using catch-up well. On a good service, the programme guide shows which programmes are available to catch up, often with a small marker, and lets you jump straight to the programme from the guide entry. How long do you get? Understanding the catch-up window

The catch-up window is the length of time a programme stays available after it airs. There is no single standard: some providers keep programmes for a day or two, others for a week, and some vary the window by channel or by type of programme. A week is a useful benchmark. It means a programme that airs on a Monday can still be watched the following Sunday, which covers most real-life situations: a late shift, a weekend away, or a week when evenings are simply too busy.

Shorter windows are not necessarily a problem, provided they are stated honestly. A one-day window can still work well if you watch most things the next evening. What causes frustration is a window that is promised and then not delivered: a programme advertised as available for seven days but gone after three, or a guide that shows a programme as available when the stream itself will not play.

Window lengthWhat it covers in practiceWho it suits

One practical tip: do not leave everything to the last day of the window. Programmes can expire at any point on their final day, and a guide that has not refreshed may still show a programme that has already gone. If a show matters to you, watch it with a day to spare.

Seven signs of a good catch-up service

1

Available on the channels you actually watch

Catch-up that only works on a few channels is of little use. Check that it covers the channels where your favourite shows air, not just a small selection.

2

A window you can rely on

The advertised window should be the real window. Programmes should stay available for the full period, and expiry times should be shown clearly in the guide.

3

Easy to find in the guide

You should be able to spot a catch-up programme at a glance and start it from the guide entry, without digging through menus or searching by hand.

4

Starts from the beginning, every time

A catch-up programme should start at the beginning, not part-way through. If it joins live by mistake, the feature is not working properly.

5

Pause, rewind and resume

Good catch-up lets you pause, rewind a missed line, and pick up where you left off. These controls are part of the point of watching on your own schedule.

6

Picture quality that matches live

A catch-up stream should look as good as the live broadcast. If it is noticeably softer or lower in resolution, the provider is cutting corners on storage.

7

Works on all your devices

Catch-up should work wherever you watch: the living-room television, a tablet in the kitchen, or a phone on the train. Test it on each device you use.

How to use catch-up, step by step

Using catch-up is straightforward once you know where to look, but the exact steps vary a little between player apps, so the guide below follows the pattern most apps share. If your app looks different, the same ideas apply: open the guide, find the programme, and play it from there.

First, open the programme guide on your device. You can usually reach it from the main menu or by pressing the guide button on your remote. Move backwards through the guide to the day and time the programme aired: most guides let you scroll back over the previous days, within the catch-up window. Look for the programme you missed. On many services, catch-up programmes are marked with a small icon or a different colour from programmes that are not available, which makes them easy to spot at a glance.

Select the programme and choose the catch-up or play option. It should start from the beginning. If it starts part-way through, or joins the live channel instead, exit and try again, and check whether your app has a separate catch-up section. Some apps keep catch-up in its own menu, separate from the live guide, and that route can be more reliable than the guide entry itself.

Catch-up, recording and on-demand: what is the difference?

These three features are often confused, because all three let you watch something other than live. They work differently, and each has its place. Catch-up is the provider keeping recent programmes for a short window after broadcast. Recording is your own device saving a programme you chose in advance, for as long as your storage allows. On-demand is a library of programmes and films that the provider keeps available for months, ready whenever you want them.

RecordingYour device saves a programme you selected in advanceUntil you delete it or run out of spaceKeeping a favourite for months, or recording a series

For most viewers, catch-up is the feature that gets used most, because it needs no planning at all. Recording is useful when you know in advance that you will miss something and want to keep it, and on-demand is best for discovering something new. A good provider offers all three, and a good player app makes it easy to tell them apart.

Frequently asked questions

Does catch-up TV cost extra?

On most services it is included in the subscription, not charged as an add-on. If a provider charges extra for it, check carefully what you are getting, and compare the total price with services that include it as standard.

Can I watch catch-up on my phone or tablet?

Yes, on a service that supports it properly. Catch-up should work on any device that can run your player app, so a programme you missed at home can be watched on the train or in another room.

What happens if a programme expires before I watch it?

Once the catch-up window ends, the programme is no longer available through catch-up. Some programmes later appear in the on-demand library, but there is no guarantee, so watch anything that matters to you with a day to spare.
